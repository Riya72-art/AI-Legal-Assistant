    How harm often unfolds — a cycle, not a single moment

    Cyberstalking, image-based abuse, domestic cruelty, and fraud rarely start as an obvious crime. They tend to follow a similar shape — small and easy to dismiss at first, then harder to ignore. Recognising the pattern early is one way to interrupt it.

    Stages 1–3 are usually how the harm builds — a stranger or someone known starts with contact that feels slightly off, then pushes for more control, access, or information, and finally the harmful act (stalking, a threat, stolen money or images, physical harm) happens.

    Stages 4–6 are the part that's easy to feel alone in — the impact on daily life, and then reaching out and rebuilding safety. You don't have to wait until things feel like a "real emergency" to move to stage 5 — reaching out earlier is not overreacting.

    The "Cycle of Abuse" — the pattern in domestic relationships

    This is a widely used model for how abuse tends to repeat inside a relationship — a calm period, tension slowly building, an incident, then a "reconciliation" phase (apologies, gifts, promises to change) that leads back to calm. Over time the calm periods often get shorter.

    Calm can feel like things are genuinely fine — which is part of why the pattern is hard to name from inside it. Tension building often shows up as walking on eggshells, criticism, or unpredictable moods.

    The incident is the abusive act itself — emotional, physical, sexual, or financial. Reconciliation can look like apologies, gifts, or promises — and it's often genuinely believed in the moment, by both people.

    If this pattern feels familiar, you're not imagining it, and it isn't your fault. The Helplines and Healing & Support pages have people who can talk this through with you.

    The laws behind these situations, in plain language.

    India's criminal laws changed on 1 July 2024 — the IPC and CrPC were replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) and Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS). This page gathers the sections most relevant to the situations covered on this site, alongside the Information Technology Act, 2000, the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, and the POCSO Act, 2012 for online and family-safety offences.
